## What the round tests

Behavioural rounds check how you work with people and make decisions under uncertainty. For AI roles they often probe a few themes in particular. Do you change course when evidence says you should? Do you take risks with user data or money seriously? Can you explain uncertainty to people who want a yes or no? Do you separate what you did from what the team did?

The best preparation is a handful of true stories you know well, told with specific numbers you can back up. Interviewers follow up, so a story you can't defend in detail will hurt you more than a modest one you can.

## Tell stories with STAR plus reflection

Use situation, task, action and result, then add a reflection at the end.

- **Situation:** one or two sentences of context. Who was involved and what was at stake.
- **Task:** what you were responsible for, specifically.
- **Action:** what you did and why, including options you rejected. Spend most of the story here.
- **Result:** what changed, with a number if you have one, and how you measured it.
- **Reflection:** what you would do differently now. Senior interviewers often care most about this part.

Aim for about two minutes, then stop and let the interviewer ask follow-ups.

## Build a story bank

Prepare five or six stories that together cover the common questions. One story can answer several questions if you shift the emphasis.

| Story to prepare | Questions it answers |
| --- | --- |
| You changed a design after seeing evaluation results | Tell me about a time data changed your mind. Describe a technical decision you're proud of. |
| You designed for a failure that prompting couldn't fix | How do you think about reliability? Tell me about a hard bug. |
| You handled an incident or a near miss | Tell me about a time something went wrong in production. |
| You disagreed with a colleague or lead | Tell me about a conflict. When did you push back? |
| You cut scope to meet a deadline | How do you prioritise? Tell me about a trade-off you made. |
| You measured whether something was worth it | How do you show impact? When did you stop or kill a project? |
| You helped someone else succeed | Tell me about mentoring. How do you raise the bar on a team? |

## Make every story verifiable

- **Keep numbers traceable.** Every figure in a story should link to a commit, a trace, an eval report or a dashboard you could show. If you don't know a number, say so and describe how you would have measured it.
- **Separate you from the team.** Say "I" for what you did and "we" for the rest, and be clear which is which.
- **Label the evidence.** Say whether something was measured in production, measured on a test set, or designed but not tested.
- **Name the limits.** Interviewers trust a story more when you say what it didn't prove.

## Using guide projects honestly

If you haven't built agents at work yet, your projects from this guide are good material. Describe them as what they are: personal learning projects, often on public or synthetic data, without real users. That framing still lets you show judgement. For example: "In my research agent project I compared a plan-first loop with a plain loop on ten questions. Planning cost 30% more and didn't improve my rubric scores, so I dropped it." Replace those numbers with your own.

Never present a learning project as production experience, and never claim results you didn't measure.

## Leadership questions for senior roles

Senior and architect loops add questions about influence, judgement and ownership. Prepare an answer for each of these:

- A teammate wants a multi-agent design and you think one agent is enough. How do you settle it?
- Leadership wants an agent launched in four weeks and you think it isn't safe. What do you do?
- An agent did something harmful in production. How do you lead the response and the follow-up?
- Nobody owns evaluation on your team. How do you change that?
- You need to migrate a working system to a new model or framework. How do you reduce the risk?
- An executive asks whether the agent "works". How do you answer when the honest answer is "mostly"?

For each, the strong answer proposes a way to decide with evidence, such as a small experiment, a staged rollout or a written decision record. It avoids winning the argument by authority.

## Questions to ask your interviewers

Your questions show what you care about and help you judge the team. Good ones for AI teams include:

- How do you decide whether a change to a prompt or model is better? Who owns the eval set?
- What happened in your last incident involving the model, and what changed afterwards?
- Which actions can your agents take without a human, and how was that line drawn?
- How do you track cost per task, and who decides when it is too high?

## Checklist

- [ ] [Must ship] Write five stories in STAR plus reflection form, each with at least one traceable number or a clear note on what wasn't measured.
- [ ] [Must ship] Map your stories to the question table so every row has at least one story.
- [ ] [Must ship] Tell two stories out loud in under two minutes each and answer one follow-up on each.
- [ ] [Recommended] Prepare an answer to each leadership question if you are applying for a senior role.
- [ ] [Recommended] Write four questions to ask interviewers, tailored to the team you are applying to.
- [ ] [Stretch] Have a friend interrupt your stories with "how do you know?" until every claim holds up.
